Where the Story Ends

Hyderabad

حیدرآباد · హైదరాబాదు

Heir of Golconda, seat of the Asaf Jahs — the city in which all the threads of the Deccan are gathered.

c. 1591

A City on the Musi

Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah left the crowded granite of Golconda — short of water, swept periodically by plague — and laid out a garden city on a grid across the Musi. At the crossing of its two great axes rose the Char Minar; beside it the vast Mecca Masjid, its central arch built with bricks fired from earth brought from the holy city itself. Whether the place was first called Bhagnagar for a dancer named Bhagmati, or simply for good fortune, is still argued in Hyderabad.

Seventeenth-century travellers — Tavernier, Thevenot — found here the diamond emporium of the world, where the stones of Kurnool and the Krishna were weighed in the shadow of the fortress five miles away.

1724 – 1948

Court of the Nizams

From 1763 the Asaf Jahi court made Hyderabad the greatest Mahomedan capital of India — the Chowmahalla and Falaknuma palaces, the mansions of the Paigah nobles, the British Residency across the river, and a court society that Gribble's editors called "quite different to what it is anywhere in India."

Under Salar Jung's thirty-year ministry the medieval city gained courts, railways, schools and hospitals — the reforms that carried the oldest state of the Deccan alive into the twentieth century.

Sources

This site retells one book, the chronicles behind it, and the modern scholarship that argues with all of them.

  • IJ. D. B. Gribble, A History of the Deccan, Vol. I — Luzac & Co., London, 1896. Dedicated to H.H. the Nizam of Hyderabad.
  • IIJ. D. B. Gribble, A History of the Deccan, Vol. II — edited and completed by Mary Pendlebury, Luzac & Co., 1924.
  • IIIFerishta's history, in Jonathan Scott's translation — Gribble's principal authority for the sultanate centuries.
  • IVElliot & Dowson, The History of India as Told by Its Own Historians — Barni, Amir Khusru, Khafi Khan and the Mughal chroniclers.
  • VThe travellers: Ibn Batuta, Nicolo Conti, Abdur Razzak, Nikitin, Varthema, Barbosa, Caesar Fredericke, Tavernier, Thevenot and Bernier.
  • VIThe Bombay Gazetteers of Kanara, Bijapur and Ahmednagar; Wilmott & Bilgrami's Sketch of the Nizam's Dominions.
  • VIIColonel Meadows Taylor, A Noble Queen and The Story of My Life; Grant Duff's Mahrattas; Orme, Wilks, Malleson and Kaye for the European century.
  • VIIIRobert Sewell, A Forgotten Empire (1900), for Paes and Nunes on Vijayanagara; the Vijayanagara Research Project of John M. Fritz and George Michell, from 1980, for what survey has since established about the city.
  • IXRichard M. Eaton, A Social History of the Deccan; George Michell & Mark Zebrowski, Architecture and Art of the Deccan Sultanates; William Dalrymple, White Mughals. These are where the modern arguments in the "In depth" boxes come from.
  • XThe Archaeological Survey of India and the UNESCO World Heritage record for dimensions, dates and conservation history; the Vilasa grant of Prolaya Nayaka for the conquest seen from the Telugu side; the Sunderlal committee report of 1948, released some fifty years after it was written.

The narrative spine of this site is condensed and adapted, with its quotations, from Gribble's two volumes — a work of 1896 whose sympathies and silences are visible throughout. Where later research contradicts him, or where the chronicles contradict each other, the disagreement is set out in the collapsible boxes rather than resolved away. Figures drawn from medieval chronicles are flagged as such; several of the most famous ones are not measurements at all.
